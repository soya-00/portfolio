# The workshop, not the tape

In June 1979, NASA Ames convened an industry workshop in San Francisco. Its proceedings were published as *Resource Management on the Flight Deck*, and it is at that meeting that the label **Cockpit Resource Management** was applied.

The workshop had an empirical basis, and it was not a recording. In January 1979 NASA published a simulator study by H. P. Ruffell Smith which put full crews through a demanding scenario in a controlled setting and observed how workload interacted with error, vigilance and decision-making. The striking result was not that individuals made mistakes; it was that crew coordination varied enormously between crews facing identical conditions, and that the variation predicted outcomes. That is an experimental finding, produced in a simulator, on crews who all walked out afterwards.

This matters for the central question more than any single accident does. The founding evidence base includes a recommendation arising from a fatal accident *and* a simulator study with no fatalities at all — and the simulator study is the one that could establish a general pattern, because it had a control condition and a denominator. An accident investigation has neither.

The pattern repeats. The first comprehensive US programme was launched by an airline in 1981, on a management-training model imported from outside aviation. The vocabulary that spread was organisational, not forensic. And the pipeline that carried it — NASA workshops, regulator recommendations, airline training departments — is institutional, with recorded evidence one input among several.

None of this establishes that the recordings did *not* drive the discipline. It establishes that a reader who is told they did should ask which part of the chain the claim is really about.

## One correction worth making

A claim circulating in some accounts holds that a 1989 accident in the United Kingdom prompted the renaming of *Cockpit* Resource Management to *Crew* Resource Management, to bring cabin crew inside the concept. Two search rounds found no source asserting it, and the chronology does not work: the canonical account places the rename in the second US generation of programmes in the mid-1980s, before that accident happened. The claim is on the cut list.
