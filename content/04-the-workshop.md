# A workshop rather than a tape

In June 1979, NASA Ames convened an industry workshop in San Francisco. Its proceedings were published as *Resource Management on the Flight Deck*, and it is at that meeting that the label **Cockpit Resource Management** was applied.

Empirical basis of that workshop was not a recording. In January 1979 NASA published a simulator study by H. P. Ruffell Smith which put full crews through a demanding scenario in a controlled setting and observed how workload interacted with error, vigilance and decision-making. Striking result concerned neither individual mistakes nor individual skill, since crew coordination varied enormously between crews facing identical conditions, and the variation predicted outcomes. That is an experimental finding, produced in a simulator, on crews who all walked out afterwards.

This matters for the central question more than any single accident does. Founding evidence base includes a recommendation arising from a fatal accident *and* a simulator study with no fatalities at all, and of the two it is the simulator study that could establish a general pattern, since it had a control condition and a denominator, neither of which an accident investigation possesses.

Pattern repeats further along. First comprehensive US program was launched by an airline in 1981, on a management-training model imported from outside aviation, and the vocabulary that spread was organizational and not forensic. A pipeline carried it, running through NASA workshops, regulator recommendations and airline training departments, is institutional, with recorded evidence one input among several.

None of this establishes that the recordings did *not* drive the discipline, although it does establish that a reader who is told they did should ask which part of the chain the claim is really about.

## One correction

A claim circulating in some accounts holds that a 1989 accident in the United Kingdom prompted the renaming of *Cockpit* Resource Management to *Crew* Resource Management, to bring cabin crew inside the concept. Two search rounds found no source asserting it, and the chronology does not work: the canonical account places the rename in the second US generation of programs in the mid-1980s, before that accident happened. Claim sits on the cut list.
