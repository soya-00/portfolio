# Before the recorder

Before there was a recording, there was a verdict.

The pattern that pre-war and post-war accident investigation produced, over and over, was *pilot error* — a finding that closed a file without requiring anyone to ask why a competent professional had done something incomprehensible. A former airline pilot turned writer, David Beaty, made the argument in 1969 that this pattern served the institutions doing the investigating: blaming the individual is administratively cheaper than examining the system that produced the individual's behaviour.

Beaty's book is real and its thesis is documentable. The sentence most often quoted from it is not: the wording that circulates in secondary retellings could not be traced to a page in either the 1969 edition or its 1991 revision, and so it does not appear here. This is the first of several places where a familiar claim turns out to have no locatable origin, and where the honest move is to say so rather than to paraphrase around the gap.

What is documented is the shape of the problem. Investigators could establish what an aircraft did — increasingly precisely, as flight data recording matured — but not why the people operating it had decided to do it. The interior of the decision was unavailable. The verdict filled the space where the explanation should have been.

The cockpit voice recorder changed what was available. It did not, by itself, change what anyone concluded.
