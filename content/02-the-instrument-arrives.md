# The instrument arrives

A cockpit voice recorder is a strange object in legal terms. It is a surveillance device that a professional is required to operate on themselves, continuously, for the duration of their working life, on the understanding that it will only ever be listened to if they are dead or nearly so.

That bargain is why the law around it diverged so sharply. Every jurisdiction had to answer the same question — *who may listen, and for what purpose* — and they answered it differently enough that the same recording is protected evidence in one country and available to a prosecutor in another.

The international baseline came from ICAO. Human factors entered the training and licensing standards of Annex 1 in 1989 and Annex 6 in 1995, and entered the investigation process through Annex 13 in 1994; the organisation's own Human Factors Training Manual is the source for that chronology. Annex 13's Standard 5.12 restricts the use of investigation records for purposes other than the investigation itself. But an ICAO Standard is a floor that states implement in their own legislation, and implementation is where the divergence lives.

By 2026 the instrument was changing again. A United States final rule published on 2 February 2026 extended required recording time from two hours to twenty-five for newly manufactured aircraft — making the recorder a record of a working day rather than of a final approach, with legal implications the existing protection regimes were not designed for.

What follows from the recorder is not a single history. It is at least four legal models operating simultaneously, and a training discipline whose relationship to any of them is less settled than the standard account suggests.
