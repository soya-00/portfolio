# The culture argument and its critics

The single most widely known claim about a cockpit voice recording is that a 1997 accident in Guam demonstrates how national culture kills — that a steep authority gradient, characteristic of the crew's nationality, prevented junior officers from challenging a captain, and that fixing the language of the cockpit fixed the airline.

That account reached an enormous readership through a popular book. It is not what the investigating authority found.

## What the authority found

The probable cause identifies the captain's inadequate briefing and execution of a non-precision approach and the first officer's and flight engineer's failure to monitor and cross-check that execution. Contributing factors: the captain's fatigue, the operator's inadequate flight crew training, and the regulator's deliberate inhibition of a ground-proximity warning system that would have covered the airport.

Two of those four elements are about an institution's training programme and a regulator's equipment decision. None of them is national culture.

## What the critics say

The published counter-position runs along three lines.

The first is factual, and it is less comfortable than either side makes it. Both junior crew members spoke. The flight engineer questioned a warning aloud; the first officer called for a missed approach. But that call came about five seconds before impact, and three seconds before it the same first officer had dismissed a sink-rate alert while the aircraft was descending at 1,400 feet per minute.

A reading built on silence does not survive that. Neither does a reading in which assertive juniors were overridden by a captain. What the record shows is slower and worse than both, and worse in a way a theory about deference does not predict.

The strongest published version of this argument reached the public through a widely read blog rather than a journal, which is worth stating plainly, because the asymmetry between a bestselling book and a blog post is part of why the cultural reading travelled further than its rebuttal.

The second is scholarly. Book-length work on the collision between Western aviation concepts and East Asian value systems argues that the framing itself imports assumptions — that the "problem" is defined by the exported model rather than discovered in the receiving culture.

The third is methodological, and it has a gap in it. The cultural reading rests on a framework of national culture dimensions whose application has been substantially criticised in management scholarship: the ecological fallacy of projecting a country-level score onto an individual's behaviour is a documented, peer-reviewed objection. But that critique is general. A critique of the *aviation* application specifically — of the step from a national score to what a particular first officer said on a particular approach — was searched for across two rounds and not located. Meanwhile the aviation replication study that tested the dimensions on flight crews concluded that they largely held.

So the honest state of the argument is: a strong general methodological objection, an aviation replication study pointing the other way, and no published work joining the two. That gap is reported here rather than filled.

The piece does not adjudicate between these readings, and the reason it cannot is instructive in its own right.
