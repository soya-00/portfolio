# Culture argument and its critics

A single claim about cockpit voice recordings is more widely known than any other: that a 1997 accident in Guam demonstrates how national culture kills, in that a steep authority gradient, characteristic of the crew's nationality, prevented junior officers from challenging a captain, and that fixing the language of the cockpit fixed the airline.

That account reached an enormous readership through a popular book. It is not what the investigating authority found.

## Authority's finding

Probable cause identifies the captain's inadequate briefing and execution of a non-precision approach and the first officer's and flight engineer's failure to monitor and cross-check that execution. Contributing factors: the captain's fatigue, the operator's inadequate flight crew training, and the regulator's deliberate inhibition of a ground-proximity warning system that would have covered the airport.

Two of those four elements are about an institution's training program and a regulator's equipment decision. None of them is national culture.

## Counter-position

Published counter-position runs along three lines.

First is factual, and it is less comfortable than either side makes it. Both junior crew members spoke. A flight engineer questioned a warning aloud, and the first officer called for a missed approach. That call came about five seconds before impact, and three seconds before it the same first officer had dismissed a sink-rate alert while the aircraft was descending at 1,400 feet per minute.

A reading built on silence does not survive that, and neither does a reading in which assertive juniors were overridden by a captain, since the record shows something slower and worse than both, and worse in a way a theory about deference does not predict.

Strongest published version of this argument reached the public through a widely read blog and not a journal, a point that should be stated plainly, because the asymmetry between a bestselling book and a blog post is part of why the cultural reading traveled further than its rebuttal.

Second is scholarly. Book-length work on the collision between Western aviation concepts and East Asian value systems argues that the framing itself imports assumptions, in that the "problem" is defined by the exported model and not discovered in the receiving culture.

Third is methodological, and it has a gap in it. Cultural reading rests on a framework of national culture dimensions whose application has been substantially criticized in management scholarship: the ecological fallacy of projecting a country-level score onto an individual's behavior is a documented, peer-reviewed objection. That critique is general, however. A critique of the *aviation* application specifically, meaning the step from a national score to what a particular first officer said on a particular approach, was searched for across two rounds and not located. Meanwhile the aviation replication study that tested the dimensions on flight crews concluded that they largely held.

So the honest state of the argument is: a strong general methodological objection, an aviation replication study pointing the other way, and no published work joining the two. That gap is reported here and not filled.

This piece does not adjudicate between these readings, and the reason it cannot is instructive in its own right.
