# Portland, 1978

On 28 December 1978, a DC-8 with a landing gear indication problem held near Portland, Oregon, until it ran out of fuel. Ten people died.

The US National Transportation Safety Board's probable cause is the most-quoted sentence in the history of this subject. It attributes the accident to the captain's failure to monitor the fuel state properly and to respond to the low fuel state and to crewmember advisories regarding it, resulting in fuel exhaustion, his inattention arising from preoccupation with the gear malfunction and preparations for a possible emergency landing. As a contributing factor, the Board identified the failure of the other two flight crewmembers either to fully comprehend the criticality of the fuel state or to communicate their concern to the captain successfully.

That contributing factor is a finding about a conversation, concerning neither a component nor a procedure nor a decision, but information that existed in the cockpit and did not travel across it. The novelty lay in the finding and not in the recording, which had existed for years, since an investigating authority was now stating in a probable cause that the distribution of understanding among a crew was causal.

The standard account compresses what came next. The Board issued a safety recommendation to the regulator, numbered **A-79-47**, not the number carried in much of the secondary literature, urging that flight crews be indoctrinated in the principles of flight deck resource management, with emphasis on participative management for captains and assertiveness training for other cockpit crewmembers. The concepts and the vocabulary of what became CRM are present in that recommendation, in 1979, in a document addressed to a regulator.

The sequence therefore runs from recording to finding, recommendation, workshop and programme, with the recording plainly among its terms. Whether that recording caused what followed, or whether it made an argument already in circulation persuasive, is the question this piece keeps open, and since Portland constitutes the strongest case for the conventional answer, the care taken here matters more than it does anywhere else.

One caution attaches to the popular version. The authorship of that recommendation is attributed, in encyclopedic and secondary sources, to a specific NTSB human factors specialist. That attribution has not been traced to a primary NTSB document, and appears here as what it is, which is widely repeated and not established.
