# A recording in law

This is the part of the story that is usually told as a footnote, and it is the part that most repays attention. There is no single legal object called a cockpit voice recording. There are at least four, and which one you are dealing with depends entirely on where the aircraft came down.

Reading the statutes in their own languages, and not through the English summaries that dominate circulation, produces a cleaner division than the literature usually acknowledges.

## Four models

**The express bar.** The statute states that investigation material may not be used as evidence. Indonesia's is the clearest instance: Article 359(1) of Law No. 1 of 2009 provides that investigation results cannot be used as evidence in judicial proceedings, and the law's own elucidation expressly names the cockpit voice recorder and its verbatim transcript among the protected records. There is no official English translation, which is part of why the strength of the Indonesian position is not well known outside the country. Canada, Brazil and the United States operate variants through different mechanisms.

**The separation principle.** The statute separates the investigation from the assignment of liability without barring the evidence. South Korea's Aviation and Railway Accident Investigation Act, Article 30, requires that accident investigation be conducted separately from judicial procedures relating to civil and criminal liability, from administrative disposition, and from administrative appeal. It is a rule about *process*, not about *admissibility*. Article 28 permits the investigating committee to withhold information at its discretion. Nowhere does the Act contain the express evidentiary bar that Indonesia's does.

The distinction is easy to lose in translation, and the Korean government's own English service is expressly non-official, so the reading here comes from the Korean text. A separation principle looks like protection while constituting a materially weaker thing.

**Discretionary control.** No aviation-specific rule, with access governed by general disclosure exemptions. Japan appears to fall here: no express evidentiary bar was located in the Japanese text of either the Transport Safety Board act or the Civil Aeronautics Act, and disclosure requests have historically run through general information-disclosure exemptions. This is an absence claim and carries the limits of one, since what can be said is that no bar was located, and not that none exists.

**Judicial seizure first.** The inverse of the express-bar model. Under Article L1621-11 of the French Code des transports, where a judicial inquiry is opened the recorders are seized by the judicial authority under the criminal procedure code, and technical investigators then take a copy of the contents under the supervision of a judicial police officer. The criminal track holds custody, while the safety investigation works from a copy. France's professional-secrecy rules bind the investigators, although the material's route into the courts is designed in and not fought over.

## Why this is the argument

Consider the same event under each. A recording made in Jakarta is statutorily inadmissible, whereas one made over France sits in a magistrate's custody before safety investigators touch it, one made in Seoul sits behind a process rule and an official's discretion, and one made in Tokyo behind a disclosure exemption.

The evidence is identical although the legal object is not, and any claim that recorded evidence *drove* a training discipline has to survive the observation that the recordings were not equally available, equally protected, or equally usable in the places that adopted it.

Two of these regimes have been tested at the top of their own legal systems, and they went opposite ways. Brazil's confidentiality provisions survived a constitutional challenge, whereas Canada's privilege was litigated to its Supreme Court on a recording itself, and the recording was ordered released into a civil claim. A statutory bar is not the same thing as a recording nobody hears.

Against that, four jurisdictions carry explicit *not documented* markers on the protection side, because an empty cell reads as *no protection* and that is not what is being reported.
