# The generations story, and who wrote it

Almost every account of CRM's history organises it into generations: a first generation of assertiveness and management style, a second broadening to crew, a third integrating technical training, a fourth folding into regulator-approved qualification programmes, a fifth reframing the whole thing around error management.

That schema comes from a 1999 paper by Helmreich, Merritt and Wilhelm, and it is the most reproduced piece of writing in the field. It is also worth noticing what it is: a periodisation written by the discipline's own principal researchers, published while the fifth generation they were describing was the one they advocated.

That is not a criticism of the work, which is careful and hedged, and the hedges are in the paper. Its authors write that the origins are *usually traced* to the 1979 workshop, conceding the conventional attribution rather than asserting it. They call the generational metaphor loose, comparing the lifespan of a CRM generation to a fruit fly's. They record a reservation that later broadening may have diluted the original focus. And on export failures they concede that training delivered from the United States went badly where the concepts were incongruent with the pilots' own culture.

An advocate's history with the hedges left in is a better source than most. But it remains a single lineage: much of the generational chronology, the cross-cultural framing and the effectiveness caveats trace to one research group.

Two consequences follow, and this piece has to state both.

The first is that no independent critical historiography of the generational schema was located. Later reviews adopt the framing descriptively rather than interrogating it. A reader who wants to know whether the generations are a real sequence or a retrospective organising device will not find that question answered in the literature, because as far as this research could establish, nobody has asked it in print.

The second is that this piece is not in a position to answer it either, and does not try. Its non-goal against original analysis applies to historiography as much as to transcripts. What it can do is show that the standard chronology is the best-documented history rather than necessarily the true one, and note that the difference between those two things is exactly the kind of thing an advocate's account is least likely to flag.

On effectiveness the founding researchers were explicit, and their position is the reason this piece makes no claim either way.
