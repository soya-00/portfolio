# Research and verification report

**Project:** CVR evidence and the making of Crew Resource Management
**Research cutoff:** 16 August 2026 · **Report compiled:** 18 August 2026
**Rounds:** one internal audit, two external verification rounds

---

## 1. What was done

Four research briefs — a CRM history, an accident corpus, a comparative law and ethics study, and an onward-reading apparatus — were audited as the sole factual basis for a public-facing publication.

**Round 1 (internal).** No retrieval. Every claim assessed against the briefs' own internal consistency, the project charter, and the overriding constraints. Produced 76 findings: 21 blocking, 44 serious, 11 minor.

**Round 2 (external).** Retrieval across all priority bands. Resolved the majority of blocking items and produced two corrections that had propagated through every brief.

**Round 3 (external, gap-closing).** Original-language statute reading, license determination per authority, and the counter-position searches that round 2 could not complete.

Governing rule throughout: **the audit may fix sources; it may not fix claims.** Locating a working URL, an open-access version, or the primary text behind a secondary summary is mechanical work with a verifiable right answer. Deciding what a claim should say once its source turns out to be wrong is editorial judgment, and an auditor that rewrites claims stops finding problems and starts smoothing them over.

## 3. Findings that changed the argument

### 3.0 Post-publication verification, 18 August 2026

Five load-bearing claims were re-checked. Full findings in `docs/VERIFICATION-2026-08-18.md`. None was contradicted; two need editorial change.

The consequential one: the divergence section states that Brazil's constitutionally tested scheme **has no counterpart elsewhere in the corpus**. Canada's section 28 privilege was litigated to the Supreme Court of Canada in *Carroll-Byrne* (2022 SCC 48, 7–2), on the cockpit voice recorder specifically, and **the recording was ordered released** into civil litigation. Not a constitutional challenge, so the narrow claim survives its wording — but an express-bar regime's bar yielding at the highest court is a more directly relevant counterpart than Brazil's, and its omission understates the comparison the piece exists to make.

Also: the Brazilian provisions protect SIPAER material generally, not the CVR specifically, so describing them as a tested *CVR protection scheme* overstates.

### 3.1 The legal taxonomy

Reading the statutes in their own languages produced a cleaner division than the English-language literature acknowledges, and it is the project's original contribution.

| Model | Jurisdictions | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Express bar** | Indonesia, Canada, Brazil, United States, EU | Statute prohibits evidentiary use |
| **Separation principle** | South Korea | Investigation procedurally separated from liability; no evidentiary bar |
| **Discretionary** | Japan | General disclosure exemptions; no aviation-specific rule located |
| **Judicial seizure first** | France | Recorders enter judicial custody before technical investigators obtain a copy |

Indonesia's Article 359(1) is the clearest express bar in the corpus, and its elucidation expressly names the cockpit voice recorder and its verbatim transcript. No official English translation exists, which is part of why the strength of the Indonesian position is not widely known.

South Korea's Article 30 is a **process rule, not an admissibility rule**. This is easy to lose in translation and the government's own English service is expressly non-official, so the finding rests on the Korean text.

### 3.2 The insurer absence

The most consequential single result. The overriding constraints require the piece to keep open the causal weight of recorded evidence against regulatory pressure, insurance pressure, manufacturer influence, union politics and institutional fashion.

Searching the scholarly literature, a safety foundation's archive, insurer primary sources and international-body material produced **no evidence that the aviation insurance industry drove CRM adoption**. The canonical histories trace every driver and never mention insurers. Where insurers discuss CRM they treat improved coordination as a *cause* of better loss experience — reversing the direction of the hypothesis.

By contrast the union role is documented: the term was published in a pilots' union magazine by the researcher who coined it, and the union was involved in the first US program. Manufacturer influence is weak and mostly later.

This absence is reported in the publication as a finding, with the reasoning made explicit: an absence produced by documentation asymmetry is not evidence of absence, and a piece that omitted the section would deliver the institutional account as though the field had been surveyed and the institutions had won.

### 3.3 Licence determinations changed the deep tier

Verified against each authority's own published terms rather than an assumption about the country. Since this project seeks no permissions, cite-only is a design constraint rather than a pending task.

| Authority | Determination |
|---|---|
| US NTSB | **Reproducible** — US Government work, 17 U.S.C. §105 |
| UK AAIB | **Reproducible** — Open Government Licence v3.0, attribution required |
| Spain CIAIAC | **Reproducible** — reuse under Ley 37/2007, attribution required |
| Germany BFU | **Cite-only** — personal and journalistic use only |
| Indonesia KNKT | **Cite-only** — no reproduction license published |
| Egypt MCA | **Cite-only** — no reproduction statement published |
| Canada (Crown) | **Cite-only** — terms not confirmed for transcript appendices |
| ICAO | **Cite-only** — purchase-only, all rights reserved |

**Two of six deep-tier cases came back cite-only.** Transcript-level treatment is available for four and not the other two, and the availability tracks the issuing state's licensing policy rather than the significance of the investigation. This is disclosed to the reader rather than hidden by quietly writing less about the affected cases.

A widespread error was corrected here: several license values had been recorded as "Spanish Gov work", "Indonesian Gov work" and so on. None of those states has a US-style public domain for official works, and none of the entries recorded actual terms.

### 3.4 Corpus eligibility resolved

An official English-language report for the 1994 Nagoya accident exists on the Japanese transport safety archive. The corpus is at 27 records against a charter floor of 25.

## 4. What was cut, and what was rescued

Ten claims examined; four cut, three rescued, three flagged and retained with the limitation visible. The full record renders to the reader as a section of the publication.

**Cut:** a quotation attributed to a 1969 book, untraceable to a page in either edition and not converted to paraphrase; the claim that a 1989 UK accident prompted the Cockpit-to-Crew renaming, unsourced and chronologically impossible; the claim that manufacturer assistance drove Chinese CRM adoption; a specific Russian carrier training figure sourced only to blogs across three rounds.

**Rescued:** the founding researchers' 1990 statement that the effectiveness question remained open, found in an open-access journal issue; Indonesian regulatory causation, re-sourced from an EU operating-ban regulation and a US category downgrade after the original material was found to rest partly on generated text; union and safety-agency statements previously quoted without attribution.

**Flagged:** the aviation-specific critique of applying national culture scores to individual cockpit conduct, searched twice and not located; the insurer role; one phrase from a 2026 appellate judgment.

One disqualification rather than a downgrade: material described in a brief as partly AI-generated cannot support a claim at any confidence level, because generated text has no author who observed anything.

## 5. The build gate

`scripts/validate.js` enforces ten rules and blocks the build on any violation.

Twelve rules after amendment; all pass. R5 originally blocked on seven quotations whose source text has never been retrieved — right in principle, wrong in placement. It failed a build that never intended to render them. The strictness moved to **R11**, which fails on any rendered reference to an unverified quotation, so the gate became stricter about rendering while ceasing to block on records nothing uses.

**R12** enforces the charter's first done-condition, which could not previously be run: `content/citations.json` maps 62 anchors to sources, each anchor unique in its section and each source resolving.

The gate caught two of its own author's mistakes on first run — an absence rendered as a negative finding, and five sources marked open access with no resolving URL. Both fixed.

Two design choices are worth recording. **R5 is a derivation proof, not a pattern match**: it proves that applying only the declared redactions and elisions to the stored text reproduces what renders, and nothing else. It does not ban square brackets or permit them. Heavy redaction warns to a human rather than failing, because a build that fails on judgment calls teaches people to disable it.

**R7 inverts the name check.** "Find the bad names" cannot be specified. "Justify every name" can. An allowlist carries each permitted name with its role and the reason it is not crew.

## 6. Reliability of the four briefs

**CRM history — good, with declared limits.** The strongest self-auditing in the set: an explicit gap list, a standalone list of widely repeated and poorly sourced claims, confidence ratings tracking documentation quality rather than importance. Weaknesses are concentration rather than error — roughly ten quotations from one advocacy-laden source, and a heavy single-lineage dependence it names but does not mitigate.

**Accident corpus — usable body, unusable source table.** Careful case analysis and an honest red team. But two entries had prose where URLs belong, five license values were country assumptions, one license was inferred from an unrelated document, one was misclassified by hosting, and nine report numbers were unverified. Rebuilt from scratch rather than patched.

**Law and ethics — the strongest research in the set.** The verification-tier system is the right instrument and the translation flagging is the model the other briefs should follow. Undercut in two places: the tier system was violated once, on the anchor provision, and nine quotation fragments across four sources carried no document-level attribution.

**Onward apparatus — would have failed the gate as written.** Seven entries could not resolve, one conflated two different books, one carried a malformed identifier that read as an unreplaced placeholder. The curation judgment is sound; the metadata beneath it was not checked.

## 8. Limitations of this research

**Anglophone documentation bias**, which the material demonstrates rather than merely asserts: every jurisdiction where a dated instrument was found reads as more developed than every jurisdiction where one was not, and that tracks language access rather than regulatory reality.

**Counterfactual invisibility.** No denominator exists. Recordings from flights where coordination worked are overwritten within hours.

**The corpus is illustrative, not representative.** Selection follows documentation availability.

**Absence claims** — the Japanese evidentiary bar, the Russian mandate, the aviation-specific Hofstede critique, the insurer role — are weaker evidence than positive findings and depend on the thoroughness of the searches performed.

**Asymmetric causal evidence.** The documented causes are institutional because institutions file documents.
