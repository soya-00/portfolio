## C045 — Korean Air 801: did the first officer speak?

**Verdict: CONFIRMED. The section still needs to change.**

He spoke, and so did the flight engineer. From the report's factual and analysis sections, in the final ninety seconds:

- About 0141:59, descending through 1,100 ft, the **first officer** said *"not in sight?"*
- About 0142:00 a GPWS "five hundred" callout; about two seconds later the **flight engineer** said *"eh?"* — the report characterises the tone as astonished
- About 0142:19, descending through 730 ft, the flight engineer called *"two hundred"* and the **first officer** said *"let's make a missed approach"*
- About one second later the flight engineer said *"not in sight"* and the first officer *"not in sight, missed approach"*

So the counter-position's empirical leg holds, and holds better than the piece currently claims: **both** junior crew members spoke, not just one.

**The qualification, which matters more than the confirmation.** Three seconds before calling for the missed approach, the same first officer dismissed a GPWS sink-rate alert with *"sink rate okay"* — while the aircraft was descending at 1,400 feet per minute. And the missed-approach call came roughly five seconds before impact.

A section that says only *"the first officer did speak"* is true and misleadingly clean. It invites a reader to conclude the junior crew were assertive and were overridden, when the record shows something more uncomfortable: they were slow, they dismissed a warning, and then they called it too late. That is evidence against the mitigated-speech reading and it is not evidence of a healthy flight deck.

**What the report itself concludes.** The probable cause is confirmed verbatim as the corpus has it — the captain's failure to adequately brief and execute the non-precision approach, and the first officer's and flight engineer's failure to effectively monitor and cross-check it; contributing, the captain's fatigue and the operator's inadequate flight crew training; contributing, the regulator's intentional inhibition of the minimum safe altitude warning system at Guam and its failure to manage the system. The report's own analysis says the crew *"seemed confused about, and did not react to"* a series of ground proximity warnings.

**No national-culture attribution appears in the probable cause or contributing factors.** That part of the corpus is correct.

**Prescribed change** — a claim decision, so handed back rather than made: section 08 should carry the timing and the sink-rate dismissal. The strongest honest version of the counter-position is not *they spoke* but *the record is worse than silence, and worse in a way the cultural reading does not predict*.

**Source status.** Retrieved via a faithful transcription of the report's Factual Information and Analysis sections. The report is a US Government work and the transcription is verbatim, but it is a transcription. Timings and wording should be confirmed against the PDF before rendering. `secondary-only` remains correct.

## C024 — Japan: is there really no express evidentiary bar?

**Verdict: CONFIRMED, moderate confidence. One citation correction.**

Nothing found in the Japanese-language material contradicts the claim, and one document positively supports the mechanism the corpus describes.

An administrative appeals record concerning a disclosure request for recorder data shows the request being handled under the **Information Disclosure Act, Article 5, items 3, 5 and 6** — general exemptions, not an aviation-specific rule. The same record shows the Transport Safety Board stating that it had returned the recorder data to its owner after publishing the investigation report and therefore did not hold it at the time of the request.

That is the discretionary model operating exactly as the corpus classifies it: no bar, control through general exemptions, and in practice control through not holding the thing at all.

**Correction to apply.** The corpus records the exemptions as *"articles 5(5) and 5(6)"*. The record shows **items 3, 5 and 6**. Item 3 is a distinct ground and its omission understates the breadth of the exemption. Applied.

**Confidence, honestly.** This remains an absence claim established without an exhaustive reading of the Civil Aeronautics Act and the Transport Safety Board establishment act. Nothing found suggests a bar exists; that is not the same as establishing that none does. The corpus already renders this as *"no express bar was located"* rather than *"none exists"*, which is the correct posture and should not be strengthened on the strength of this check.

## C027 / C132 — Brazil: constitutional challenge

**Verdict: CONFIRMED as to outcome. Two overstatements and three factual corrections.**

The judgment is real and the outcome is as recorded. The Supreme Federal Court dismissed the direct action of unconstitutionality as unfounded and declared articles 88-C, 88-D, 88-I §2, 88-K, 88-N and 88-P of the aeronautical code, as amended in 2014, constitutional.

### Three corrections, applied

1. **The judgment date is 14 August 2024**, not September 2024 as the report records. The case was moved to the head of the docket following a fatal accident five days earlier.
2. **Justice Flávio Dino was partially dissenting.** The report currently lists a run of concurring justices and records no dissent. It was decided by majority, not unanimously.
3. **The relator's proposed disposition dates from a virtual session of 27 August to 3 September 2021**, after which another justice requested time to examine the case. That is the source of the September date, which attached to the wrong event.

### Two overstatements, handed back

**First: it is a SIPAER scheme, not a CVR scheme.** The upheld provisions govern confidentiality of Aeronautical Accident Investigation and Prevention System material — voluntary contributions, investigators' analyses and conclusions, custody of items of interest, and the requirement of judicial requisition before that material is used as evidence. The court's reasoning turns on preserving the relationship of trust between contributors and the prevention authority.

The corpus uses this to say Brazil's **CVR protection** has been constitutionally tested. That is an extension the judgment does not clearly support. Cockpit voice recordings sit inside the protected class, but the provisions the court examined are not CVR provisions and the reasoning is about voluntary reporting.

**Second, and more serious: "no counterpart elsewhere in the corpus" is misleading.**

Canada's section 28 CVR privilege was tested in **Canada (Transportation Safety Board) v. Carroll-Byrne, 2022 SCC 48**, decided 25 November 2022, a 7–2 judgment of the Supreme Court of Canada. It arose from Air Canada Flight 624's landing accident at Halifax in March 2015. The manufacturer sought the recording under section 28(6); the Board opposed, claiming statutory privilege; the airline and the pilots' association opposed on privacy grounds. The motion judge listened to the recording in camera, found it reliable, relevant and necessary, held that the public interest in the proper administration of justice outweighed the privilege, and **ordered it released**. The Court of Appeal affirmed. The Supreme Court dismissed the Board's appeal.

This is not a constitutional challenge, so the narrow claim survives on its wording. But the framing does not. Canada's protection scheme was tested at its highest court, **on the cockpit voice recorder specifically**, and the recording was ordered disclosed into civil litigation.

For a piece whose spine is a comparison of protection regimes, that is a more directly relevant counterpart than Brazil's — it shows an express-bar jurisdiction's bar yielding in practice, which is exactly the kind of thing the taxonomy risks obscuring. Omitting it while claiming Brazil has no counterpart understates the comparison.

**One incidental result.** The register's entry UM-06 flagged the onward section's line that section 28 is *"the most litigated provision in this area"* as an unsourced evaluative claim. Carroll-Byrne and its two-level appellate history make it considerably more defensible than I judged. It should move from *cut or replace* to *retain and cite*.

**Prescribed** — both overstatements are claim decisions. Either narrow the Brazil claim to the SIPAER scheme and add Canada as a counterpart, or drop the uniqueness framing.

## C034 / C035 — Flash Airlines 604

**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and stronger than the corpus states.**

The disagreement is better documented and more formal than *"disagreed publicly"* conveys.

The Egyptian Ministry of Civil Aviation's final report, released 25 March 2006, **did not state a probable cause at all**. It listed four possible causes, one of which was a technical failure leading to disorientation.

The United States authority responded with a document reported at around forty pages, stating that the evidence strongly supports the conclusions that no aircraft-related failure caused the accident and that it is explained by the captain's spatial disorientation and the first officer's failure to assume timely control. It rejected the equal-possibility framing of the decision tree, and stated that the decision trees had been produced independently by the Egyptian authority rather than jointly. It cited the recording as showing twenty-four seconds elapsing after the aircraft banked before corrective input began.

The French authority issued a separate statement identifying errors and omissions.

**The disagreement was squarely about the recorded evidence** — what the flight data and cockpit voice records showed about crew inputs and their timing. That is precisely the corpus's point, and the case supports it more strongly than written.

**Two additions the corpus does not have.**

The French authority noted that CRM training was not mandatory in Egypt at the time and that the operator had no CRM programme. That is directly on the piece's subject and is currently absent.

The record also supports a reading that the junior pilot did not effectively challenge the captain — which makes this a second corpus case bearing on the authority-gradient question, alongside Guam.

**C035 stands.** No copyright, reproduction or terms-of-use statement for Egyptian accident reports was located. The absence is unchanged and the cite-only determination holds. Note that the report is reachable through the French authority's archive, which is how the corpus already records it.

**One discrepancy to resolve.** Sources differ on the passenger and crew split — 142 passengers and 6 crew in some, 135 and 13 in others. Both total 148, which is the figure the corpus carries, so the headline number is safe and the split is not.

## C036 — Dryden: the widest frame

**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and stronger than written. It also surfaces a quotation problem.**

The Commission issued **191 recommendations, all of which were accepted**. It is described in the safety and healthcare-safety literature as a landmark, and is credited with shifting Canadian practice away from individual-blame investigation and toward the systemic approach that later became safety management systems.

Its organisational scope is as the corpus describes and wider: commercial deregulation and competitive pressure, the airline's management decisions and scheduling, the decision to route the aircraft through an airport without appropriate ground equipment, the unserviceable auxiliary power unit that prevented de-icing with an engine running, absent manuals and inadequate de-icing training, and the regulator's oversight. A multidisciplinary team including human-factors specialists interviewed over 160 witnesses across roughly twenty months.

**CRM training appears among the recommendations.** The corpus does not record this and should.

### The quotation problem

The Commission's own conclusion, as reproduced in secondary sources including the US regulator's lessons-learned page, runs in two halves. The first assigns responsibility to the captain as pilot-in-command for the decision to land and take off. The second states that it is equally clear that the air transportation system failed him by allowing him to be placed in a situation where he did not have the tools to make the proper decision.

**The corpus's pending quotation `q-moshansky-pilot-in-command` captures only the first half.** Rendered alone, it reverses the Commission's meaning — it reads as an individual-blame finding from the inquiry most celebrated for refusing exactly that.

This is the sharpest practical illustration of why the ellipsis convention exists. Whoever performs the verbatim capture must take both halves or neither.

**On "the widest frame any authority applied."** Defensible, and still a characterisation rather than a sourced finding. It is the project's comparative judgement across its own corpus, and nobody published it. It belongs in the unmapped register alongside the taxonomy. **Added as UM-15.**
